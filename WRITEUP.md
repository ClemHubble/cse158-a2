# Detecting AI-generated choreography in Dance Dance Revolution step charts

## Abstract

This project addresses the binary classification problem of distinguishing between human-authored and AI-generated Dance Dance Revolution (DDR) step charts. We evaluate three distinct methodological approaches that vary in their degree of implicit learning: explicit feature engineering using domain knowledge, semi-implicit n-gram frequency analysis, and fully implicit sequence modeling via recurrent neural networks. Our experiments demonstrate that hand-crafted features achieve the highest classification performance on our dataset of approximately 2,200 charts, suggesting that the systematic biases introduced by the Dance Dance Convolution model are efficiently captured by targeted statistical summaries rather than requiring end-to-end learned representations.

## Introduction

Dance Dance Revolution is a rhythm game where players step on directional arrows synchronized with music. Each song's choreography, known as a "step chart," specifies the precise timing and direction of each step. Professional step chart authoring requires significant expertise, balancing musical interpretation, physical playability, and difficulty progression.

Dance Dance Convolution (DDC), introduced by Donahue et al. (2017), represents the first application of deep learning to automatic step chart generation. The system operates in two stages: a step placement network determines when steps should occur based on audio features, while a step selection network determines which arrow configuration to use at each placement. While DDC produces playable charts, experienced players report that the output feels "mechanical" or "artificial" compared to human-authored choreography.

This observation motivates our central research question: can we systematically detect whether a given step chart was authored by a human or generated by DDC? Such a classifier would have practical applications in quality assurance for rhythm game content and could inform improvements to generative choreography systems by identifying their characteristic failure modes.

## Dataset

### Data sources

Our dataset comprises step charts from two primary sources. The human-authored charts originate from the official In The Groove series (ITG and ITG2) and the Fraxtil community packs (Arrow Arrangements, Beast Beats, and Tsunamix III). These represent professional-quality choreography widely regarded as exemplary within the DDR community. The AI-generated charts were produced by running the DDC inference pipeline on the same audio files, ensuring a paired comparison where the only variable is the authorship method.

### Preprocessing pipeline

The raw step chart data underwent several preprocessing stages documented across four preliminary notebooks. The first notebook normalizes the human chart format from the native StepMania `.sm` format into a standardized JSON representation. Each chart is represented as a sequence of step events, where each event contains a timestamp (in seconds) and a four-character pattern string encoding the arrow states. The pattern string follows the DDR convention where each character represents one of the four arrows (left, down, up, right) and takes values of `0` (no input), `1` (tap), `2` (hold begin), or `3` (hold release).

The second notebook generates corresponding AI charts by invoking the DDC Docker container on each audio file. The DDC output, originally in StepMania format, is parsed and converted to the same JSON schema. The third notebook addresses a temporal alignment issue: DDC outputs timestamps in beat units rather than seconds, requiring conversion using each song's tempo metadata. The fourth notebook performs exploratory data analysis, examining distributional differences between human and AI charts.

After filtering charts with fewer than 10 steps (insufficient for meaningful feature extraction), the final dataset contains 1,110 human charts and 1,110 AI charts spanning 222 unique songs. Each song contributes multiple charts at different difficulty levels (Beginner, Easy, Medium, Hard, Challenge), yielding approximately 5 charts per song per authorship type.

### Train-test split methodology

A critical consideration in our experimental design is preventing data leakage through song-level information. Because multiple charts derive from the same underlying audio (at different difficulties), a naive random split could place training and test charts from the same song in both partitions. The model might then exploit audio-specific characteristics rather than learning generalizable authorship signatures.

We address this by performing the train-test split at the song level rather than the chart level. Songs are randomly partitioned with 80% allocated to training and 20% to testing. This ensures that all charts from a given song appear exclusively in either the training or test set, never both. The resulting split yields approximately 1,760 training charts and 440 test charts, maintaining the balanced class distribution of the original dataset.

## Methodology

Our investigation systematically varies the degree to which the model must discover discriminative patterns from raw data versus relying on researcher-specified features. We organize this spectrum into three approaches: explicit feature engineering, semi-implicit n-gram analysis, and fully implicit sequence modeling.

### Approach 1: explicit feature engineering

The explicit approach represents the traditional machine learning paradigm where domain expertise guides feature construction. We define eight numerical features that capture aspects of step chart structure theorized to differ between human and AI authorship.

The first feature, steps per second, measures overall chart density by dividing the total step count by the chart duration. Human charters often vary density to match musical intensity, while DDC's onset detection may produce more uniform density. The second and third features, mean and standard deviation of inter-step time intervals, characterize rhythmic consistency. Human charts frequently employ rubato and tempo variations absent in DDC's fixed-BPM output.

The fourth feature, hold ratio, computes the fraction of steps involving hold notes (pattern characters `2` or `3`). DDC's step selection network treats holds independently at each timestep, potentially producing different hold distributions than humans who plan hold patterns holistically. The fifth feature, jump ratio, measures the proportion of steps requiring simultaneous input on two or more arrows. Jump placement requires ergonomic consideration that DDC does not explicitly model.

The sixth feature, unique pattern ratio, divides the count of distinct step patterns by total steps, measuring vocabulary diversity. DDC's pattern selection is conditioned only on recent history and difficulty, possibly underutilizing rare but musically appropriate patterns. The seventh feature, repeat ratio, computes how often consecutive steps use identical patterns. Human charters often avoid or deliberately employ repetition for musical effect, while DDC's Markov-like generation may produce different repetition statistics.

The eighth feature, arrow entropy, quantifies the balance of arrow usage across the four directions. We compute the Shannon entropy of the arrow frequency distribution, where uniform usage yields maximum entropy. Human charters may favor certain arrows for ergonomic or stylistic reasons, while DDC's selection mechanism may produce different balance characteristics.

These eight features are computed for each chart and standardized to zero mean and unit variance before model training. We evaluate three classification algorithms: logistic regression serves as an interpretable linear baseline, random forest captures nonlinear interactions while providing feature importance rankings, and gradient boosting offers an alternative ensemble method often achieving state-of-the-art performance on tabular data. Hyperparameters are set to standard values (100 trees, maximum depth 10 for random forest; 100 estimators, maximum depth 5 for gradient boosting) without extensive tuning, as our primary interest is comparing approaches rather than maximizing absolute performance.

### Approach 2: semi-implicit n-gram features

The n-gram approach reduces researcher assumptions by extracting features directly from the step pattern vocabulary without requiring domain knowledge about what statistics matter. We compute three categories of features that the model can weight according to their discriminative power.

Unigram features count the normalized frequency of each unique step pattern appearing in a chart. For example, a feature might indicate that pattern `0001` (right arrow only) comprises 12% of the chart's steps. These frequencies capture which individual patterns are favored or avoided by each authorship type without the researcher specifying which patterns matter.

Bigram features count the normalized frequency of consecutive pattern pairs, capturing transition preferences. A feature might encode that the transition from `0001` to `0010` (right to up) occurs at a certain rate. Bigrams capture the Markov structure of step sequences, potentially revealing differences in how human and AI charters construct pattern flows.

Delta-time histogram features discretize the distribution of inter-step timing into 20 bins spanning 0 to 2 seconds. Each bin's density becomes a feature, capturing timing characteristics without requiring the researcher to specify summary statistics like mean or variance. The model learns which timing regions are discriminative.

The combined feature set typically comprises 800-1000 dimensions depending on the vocabulary size in the training set. Features are sparse (most patterns appear in few charts) and high-dimensional relative to the explicit approach. We apply the same three classification algorithms, with logistic regression using L2 regularization (C=0.1) to prevent overfitting in the high-dimensional space.

### Approach 3: fully implicit sequence modeling

The sequence modeling approach represents maximum implicit learning: we provide raw step sequences and let a neural network discover all relevant patterns through end-to-end training. Each chart is represented as a sequence of integer-encoded step patterns, padded or truncated to a fixed length of 300 steps.

The architecture employs a bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, chosen for its ability to capture long-range dependencies in sequential data. The model begins with an embedding layer that maps the discrete pattern vocabulary (approximately 40-50 unique patterns) into a 32-dimensional continuous space. Two stacked bidirectional LSTM layers with 64 and 32 hidden units respectively process the embedded sequence, allowing the model to incorporate both forward and backward context when forming representations.

Dropout regularization (rate 0.3) after each LSTM layer combats overfitting. A dense layer with 32 units and ReLU activation provides a learned representation, followed by a single sigmoid output for binary classification. The model is trained with binary cross-entropy loss using the Adam optimizer, with early stopping based on validation loss (patience of 5 epochs) to prevent overfitting.

This approach makes minimal assumptions about which patterns or statistics matter. The embedding layer can learn arbitrary pattern representations, the LSTMs can learn complex temporal dependencies, and the final layers can combine these into arbitrary decision functions. However, this flexibility comes at the cost of requiring sufficient training data to discover useful representations.

## Results

### Classification performance

The explicit feature approach achieved the highest performance across all three algorithms. Random forest attained 88.9% accuracy and 0.961 ROC-AUC, with gradient boosting achieving similar results (88.0% accuracy, 0.959 ROC-AUC). Even the linear logistic regression model reached 86.8% accuracy and 0.948 ROC-AUC, suggesting that the eight hand-crafted features capture approximately linearly separable structure.

The n-gram approach achieved competitive but slightly lower performance. Random forest reached 86.8% accuracy and 0.951 ROC-AUC, while gradient boosting achieved 87.5% accuracy and 0.953 ROC-AUC. Logistic regression, despite operating in the high-dimensional space, achieved 85.2% accuracy and 0.940 ROC-AUC. The performance gap relative to explicit features is modest, indicating that n-gram statistics do capture relevant authorship signals, though less efficiently than targeted domain features.

The LSTM sequence model achieved the lowest performance at 79.4% accuracy and 0.862 ROC-AUC. While substantially above chance (50%), this represents a significant gap compared to the feature-based approaches. The training curves revealed that validation accuracy plateaued around epoch 10 while training accuracy continued improving, suggesting the model was beginning to memorize training examples rather than learning generalizable patterns.

### Cross-validation stability

To verify that test set performance was not an artifact of a fortunate random split, we performed 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training data. The explicit feature models achieved stable cross-validation ROC-AUC scores: logistic regression at 0.937 ± 0.018, random forest at 0.948 ± 0.015, and gradient boosting at 0.945 ± 0.016. The low standard deviations across folds indicate that performance generalizes across different data subsets.

### Feature importance analysis

Examining which features drive classification reveals interpretable patterns consistent with known DDC characteristics. In logistic regression, the hold ratio coefficient was strongly negative (associated with human authorship), aligning with DDC's documented difficulty in producing natural hold patterns. The standard deviation of inter-step timing was also negative, consistent with DDC's fixed-tempo output lacking human rhythmic variation.

Random forest importance rankings placed hold ratio and timing standard deviation as the two most important features, reinforcing the logistic regression findings. The unique pattern ratio ranked third, suggesting that vocabulary diversity provides independent signal beyond timing and hold statistics. Arrow entropy and jump ratio contributed moderately, while steps per second and repeat ratio showed lower importance.

For the n-gram approach, the most important unigram features were patterns involving holds (`0020`, `0002`, `2000`) and jumps (`0101`, `1010`), echoing the explicit features' emphasis on these characteristics. The most discriminative bigrams often involved transitions to or from hold states. Timing histogram bins in the 0.1-0.3 second range showed highest importance, corresponding to typical step intervals where human rhythmic variation manifests.

### Error analysis

Examining misclassified charts revealed patterns in model failures. False positives (human charts classified as AI) tended to be simpler charts at Beginner or Easy difficulty where limited pattern vocabulary reduced the statistical signal. These charts contain fewer holds and jumps, making them superficially similar to DDC output which also underutilizes these elements.

False negatives (AI charts classified as human) often came from songs with irregular rhythmic structure where DDC's step placement happened to align well with the audio. Charts where the song tempo matched DDC's assumptions showed fewer timing artifacts, reducing the discriminative power of timing-based features.

The error rate varied by difficulty level, with higher-difficulty charts (Hard, Challenge) achieving better classification accuracy. This aligns with expectations: complex charts provide more statistical evidence for discrimination, while simple charts with limited vocabulary offer less signal.

## Discussion

### Why explicit features outperform implicit learning

The superior performance of hand-crafted features over implicit approaches may seem counterintuitive given deep learning's success in domains like computer vision and natural language processing. Several factors explain this result in our setting.

First, the dataset size of approximately 1,760 training charts is modest by deep learning standards. Sequence models with learned embeddings require substantial data to discover useful representations, and our training set may provide insufficient coverage of the pattern space. The LSTM's millions of parameters create a high-capacity function space that is difficult to navigate without abundant examples.

Second, the discriminative signal is concentrated in specific statistical properties that domain knowledge directly targets. DDC's architectural choices create systematic biases in hold handling, rhythmic variation, and pattern selection that manifest clearly in aggregate statistics. Explicit features efficiently compress this information, while implicit approaches must discover the same regularities through gradient-based optimization.

Third, the sequential structure of step charts differs from natural language or audio where LSTMs excel. Step patterns are largely conditionally independent given the audio and difficulty, reducing the value of long-range temporal modeling. The Markov structure captured by bigram features may approximate the relevant dependencies sufficiently, making deeper sequence models unnecessary.

### Interpreting the n-gram results

The n-gram approach achieved intermediate performance, suggesting partial value in automatic feature discovery. The ~800-dimensional feature space contains redundant and irrelevant features alongside discriminative ones, requiring regularization to prevent overfitting. The random forest's ability to select relevant features contributed to its strong performance despite the dimensionality.

Notably, n-gram features captured similar information to explicit features through a different lens. Rather than computing a single "hold ratio" statistic, the n-gram approach includes separate features for each hold-involving pattern, allowing the model to weight them differentially. This flexibility could reveal nuances missed by aggregate statistics, though our results suggest the aggregate summaries are sufficient for classification.

### Limitations and future work

Several limitations qualify our findings. The DDC model evaluated represents a specific architecture from 2017; modern generative approaches may produce charts with different characteristic signatures. Our binary classification framing treats human authorship as monolithic, though different human charters exhibit individual styles that could confound analysis. The feature set, while interpretable, may miss other discriminative aspects such as musical structure alignment or difficulty curve analysis.

Future work could address these limitations through several extensions. Collecting DDC outputs from multiple architecture variants or training checkpoints would test whether our findings generalize across AI choreography systems. Per-charter analysis could reveal whether individual human styles are distinguishable and whether some styles are more "AI-like" than others. Incorporating audio features would enable studying the alignment between step placement and musical events.

## Conclusion

We presented a systematic comparison of three approaches for detecting AI-generated DDR step charts: explicit feature engineering, semi-implicit n-gram analysis, and fully implicit sequence modeling. Our experiments on 2,220 charts from professional sources demonstrate that hand-crafted features capturing domain-relevant statistics achieve the highest classification performance (88.9% accuracy, 0.961 ROC-AUC), outperforming both high-dimensional n-gram features and LSTM sequence models.

These results suggest that the Dance Dance Convolution model introduces systematic biases that are efficiently detected through targeted statistical analysis. The most discriminative features relate to hold note handling, rhythmic variation, and pattern vocabulary diversity, aligning with qualitative observations from experienced players about DDC's characteristic artifacts.

From a machine learning perspective, our findings illustrate that implicit learning approaches require sufficient data and appropriate inductive biases to outperform domain-informed feature engineering. When discriminative signal is concentrated in specific aggregate statistics, explicit feature extraction remains a powerful and interpretable approach.
